=====================================================================================

THESIS
This research develops a risk-aware framework for automated DICOM-to-3D anatomical
reconstruction that quantifies stage-wise geometric error, predicts final-mesh reliability
without ground truth, and selects reconstruction pipelines subject to task-specific
accuracy and computational constraints.

CONTEXT
MEDSYS already runs end to end (Section 1), so the contribution is not the platform but
the reliability of its output: MEASURE -> PREDICT -> DECIDE.

METHOD AND LIMITS
About 30 web searches (October 2026), a code audit of the repository at commit 130686f,
two external reviews, and direct reading of selected primary sources, marked (+).
Unmarked references come from search summaries or background knowledge and must be
verified before citing. This is a scoping note; a logged systematic search must confirm
novelty.


1. CURRENT STATE OF MEDSYS

CT: intensity-threshold heuristic or TotalSegmentator [1], built on nnU-Net [2], run in
its 3 mm fast mode by default.
MRI: N4, denoising, BET and box-prompted MedSAM refinement of the BET mask (when the
checkpoint is present), then Gaussian-mixture tissue classes.
PET: tumour-to-background hotspot.
Every route meshes through one function: Gaussian blur of the mask, marching cubes,
Taubin smoothing (30 iterations) and decimation, with blur and decimation strengths
hand-tuned per structure (sigma 0.5-1.0, reduction 0.3-0.6).
Validation to date: Dice, IoU, HD95 and ASSD of the heuristic engine against
TotalSegmentator in fast mode on CPU, on one chest CT (lungs 0.95, skeleton 0.11). This measures agreement
with TotalSegmentator, not anatomical accuracy; it is pilot evidence motivating the
research problem, not evidence about either engine.


2. SCIENTIFIC GAPS AND RESEARCH QUESTIONS

A1 - Stage-wise attribution of geometric error
  Error sources in patient-specific 3D models are well characterised, with segmentation
  error dominating printing error [3, 4], and smoothing is known to alter geometry [5].
  There is limited evidence for automated, quantitative, stage-wise attribution in
  end-to-end DICOM-to-mesh pipelines that compares segmentation engines and
  reconstruction choices, including their interactions, across multiple structures
  against expert ground truth.
  RQ1 (MEASURE): Which pipeline stages, and which interactions between them, contribute
  most to final anatomical geometric error?

A2 - Ground-truth-free, task-specific mesh reliability
  Segmentation-level quality control without ground truth is crowded [6, 7, 8]. Less
  explored is bounding final-mesh error. Operationally: predict an upper bound U(x) for
  ASSD, HD95 and volume error such that P(E_mesh <= U(x)) >= 1 - alpha holds empirically
  on held-out cases, using split conformal prediction [9]; task suitability is then
  P(E_mesh <= tau_task) for VR, measurement or printing. A2 differs materially from
  segmentation QC only if RQ1 shows that reconstruction stages or topology contribute
  non-trivially.
  RQ2 (PREDICT): Can final-mesh error and task suitability be predicted without ground
  truth at inference time, with empirically calibrated coverage?

A3 - Accuracy-constrained pipeline selection
  Adaptive inference and deferral exist [10, 11, 12], so routing easy cases to a cheap
  engine is not novel. The open formulation chooses engine and reconstruction settings p
  that minimise cost subject to a reliability target, with energy [13] as a cost term
  rather than the thesis:

      p* = argmin_p [ energy(p) + lambda * latency(p) ]
           subject to  P( E_mesh(p) <= tau_task ) >= 1 - alpha

  RQ3 (DECIDE): Can RQ2's bounds select the lowest-cost pipeline that satisfies a
  predefined task-specific reliability constraint?
  Priority: RQ1 is the core thesis and stands on its own; RQ2 is the major extension and
  RQ3 the applied extension, each built only on its predecessor's results.


3. REFERENCE STANDARD

- Three distinct objects:
    Ground truth: an expert-annotated mask from a public dataset. It is a reference
      standard rather than truth, since inter-rater variability sets a floor [14].
    Reference surface: a mesh made from that mask by one fixed, documented procedure
      (marching cubes at native resolution, no smoothing or decimation).
    Test mesh: MEDSYS output under configuration p.
  Error is always test mesh against reference surface.
- Tier 1, digital phantoms: analytic surfaces voxelised at the study's spacings. This
  isolates reconstruction error exactly and measures the reference procedure's own
  error, which becomes the floor below which Tier 2 differences are not interpretable.
- Tier 2, human anatomy: end-to-end error against expert annotations.


4. STUDY DESIGN

- Structures by regime, CT first: high contrast (lung, bone), low contrast (liver) and,
  optionally, thin structures (vessels), from public expert-labelled datasets
  [15, 16, 17]; brain MRI is an extension once RQ1 runs on CT, and PET is out of scope
  (no labelled PET ground truth).
  Leakage and label bias: evaluate TotalSegmentator only on a held-out split or external
  data, and check how each dataset's labels were made, since model-assisted labels can
  favour the model family that produced them. Licences differ by dataset.
- Configurations:
    Production parameters (what MEDSYS ships: TotalSegmentator fast mode, blur and
      decimation hand-tuned per structure) appear in results as one named configuration
      only.
    Research experiments use one uniform grid for every structure, with no
      per-structure tuning.
    Canonical baseline: TotalSegmentator at 1.5 mm, native spacing, no blur, marching
      cubes, no smoothing, no decimation. Every effect is reported as a change from it.
      With the expert mask in place of the engine, it is exactly the reference-surface
      procedure.
- Exp. A, segmentation: heuristic, TotalSegmentator 3 mm (fast) and TotalSegmentator
  1.5 mm (full) against expert labels; BET+MedSAM only in the MRI extension.
- Exp. B, reconstruction (causal isolation): hold the segmentation fixed and intervene
  only on spacing and four explicit factors, all fixed before analysis:
    mask blur sigma in {0, 0.5, 1} mm (millimetres, so blur is not confounded with
      spacing)
    isosurface algorithm (two variants)
    Taubin smoothing iterations in {0, 10, 30, 60}
    decimation in {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75}
  Run on the expert mask (pure reconstruction effect) and on each engine's mask (does
  reconstruction amplify or dampen segmentation error?).
- Exp. C, full pipeline: DICOM -> engine -> mesh against the reference surface.
- Exp. D, attribution, split-plot design [18]: expensive whole-plot factors are engine x
  resolution (3x4 = 12 segmentations per case); cheap sub-plot factors are blur x
  isosurface x smoothing x decimation (3x2x4x4 = 96 meshes per segmentation), so 1,152
  meshes per case-structure. Unless the pilot shows the full set is affordable, run the
  full grid on a subset (e.g. 20 cases) and the canonical and production configurations
  on all cases. Fit a mixed-effects model with
  case and structure as random effects, and compute Sobol indices for the continuous
  reconstruction parameters [19]. Time a 2-3 case pilot first; if the full design is too
  costly, fall back to a fractional factorial that keeps two-factor interactions
  unaliased (resolution V) or a response-surface design.
- Sample size is set by RQ2. At alpha = 0.05, split conformal prediction needs at least
  19 calibration cases per group for a finite bound, and stable coverage needs more [9].
  With per-structure calibration plus training and test splits, plan on roughly 60-100
  cases per structure, to be confirmed against pilot variability. For RQ1, choose n so
  that the confidence-interval half-width h on mean ASSD is acceptable:
  n ~= (1.96 s / h)^2, e.g. a pilot SD s = 1 mm and h = 0.3 mm gives about 43
  (illustrative).
- Accuracy and cost are separate experiments. Experiments A-D measure geometry only and
  do not depend on hardware. Latency and energy are measured in a separate benchmark on
  fixed, documented hardware and combined only in RQ3. The device is recorded with every
  run, since GPU non-determinism can perturb segmentations slightly.
- Metrics, chosen per Metrics Reloaded [14], reported per case with confidence
  intervals: surface (ASSD, HD95, surface Dice [20], Chamfer), geometric (volume and
  surface-area error) and topological (components, holes, self-intersections). Task
  tolerances come from literature or clinicians and are fixed before analysis.
- Output: every mesh ships with a JSON or glTF-extras quality report giving U(x) and
  P(E_mesh <= tau_task) for each task, since STL cannot carry metadata.

MAIN RISKS AND MITIGATIONS
- Size of the design space: pilot timing; full grid on a subset, or a fractional design.
- Too few independent expert-labelled cases for RQ2: CT-first scope; pooled public
  datasets.
- Reference-standard discipline: one fixed, documented procedure; Tier 1 floor.
- Scope creep: RQ1 first; MRI only as an extension; PET out.


5. ENGINEERING PREREQUISITES (not contributions)

Expert-ground-truth validation harness; a configuration layer separating production
defaults from research grids (reconstruction parameters are currently hard-coded at each
call site); GPU inference (the installed PyTorch build is
CPU-only); benchmarking BET+MedSAM against HD-BET [21]; verified de-identification and
opaque dataset identifiers [22], since datasets are currently named from upload
filenames; a provenance record (model version, weights hash, commit) on every output;
multi-series handling.


6. COMMERCIALISATION AND DEPLOYMENT CONSTRAINTS

Diagnostic-use anatomical models are expected to come from cleared software held by
incumbents [23], so early use is teaching, research and non-diagnostic planning. Several
TotalSegmentator subtasks need a commercial licence, while "total" and "total_mr" are
Apache-2.0 [24]. EU high-risk AI duties for AI in medical devices apply from 2 Aug 2028
[25]; the FDA's AI-enabled device list is dominated by radiology [26], and a June 2026
rule classifies radiological ML-based quantitative imaging software [27]. A student
project can produce regulatory-ready documentation, not clearance.
